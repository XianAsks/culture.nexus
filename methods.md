## 1. The comparative problem

### 1.1 Smith's charge

J. Z. Smith reviewed four centuries of scholarship comparing early Christianity with the religions of late antiquity and found the results tracked an underlying Protestant–Catholic polemic. Comparisons that appeared disinterested were doing confessional work: Protestant scholars isolating a "primitive Christianity" from its pagan surroundings, Catholic scholars finding continuity. The scholarship was good; the conclusions were pre-loaded.

The relevance is direct. This project's founding observation is *"the rainbow body looks like the resurrection,"* and it carries a stated parenthetical motive about the historical versus ecclesiastical Jesus. Both are exactly the conditions under which Smith's mechanism operates.

### 1.2 Three kinds of resemblance

Every comparative claim must declare which of these it is making. The `#r/` facet enforces it.

| Claim | Asserts | Requires |
|---|---|---|
| **Analogy** | Structural or phenomenological similarity. No contact claimed. | A defensible description of both terms. Nothing more. |
| **Genealogy** | One derives from the other, by contact or descent. | A route, a date, and a mechanism. See §5. |
| **Homology** | Both descend from a shared third source. | Identification of the source, plus routes to both. |

Most alleged Buddhist–Christian parallels are analogies dressed as genealogies. Some — the halo is the working example — are probably homologies mistaken for genealogies. Getting the type right is most of the analysis; the evidence bar differs by an order of magnitude between rows.

### 1.3 Two further rules from Smith

- **Prefer multi-term comparison.** Two-term comparison (Dzogchen ↔ Christianity) invites pattern-matching, because with only two terms every shared feature looks significant. Adding Manichaeism, Bön, Chan, Hesychasm and Zoroastrianism as further terms makes a feature shared by all of them look like what it is: common, and therefore unremarkable.
- **Attend to difference, not to uniqueness or identity.** "These are the same" and "this is unique" are both conversation-enders. The productive form is: *these are similar in this respect and differ in that one, and the difference is patterned.*

## 2. Emic and etic

### 2.1 Origin: an analogy from phonetics

Kenneth Pike coined the pair in 1954 by clipping **phon-etic** and **phon-emic**.

- **Phonetic**: every sound a human vocal tract can produce, on a grid that applies to all languages equally and can be instrumentally measured. Imposed from outside; comparable across systems.
- **Phonemic**: the sound distinctions that *function* within one language — the ones its speakers hear as different. English distinguishes /l/ from /r/; Japanese does not. English has aspirated and unaspirated /p/ (*pin*, *spin*) but speakers hear one sound; in Korean that difference is phonemic and carries meaning.

Generalised: **emic** categories are those that do work inside a system as its participants operate it. **Etic** categories are the analyst's grid, imposed from outside so that different systems can be compared.

A detail usually lost: **for Pike, etic was scaffolding and emic was the goal.** The external grid is a provisional way in, used until the system's own joints can be found. Emic description was the achievement.

### 2.2 Harris inverted the valence

Marvin Harris made etic the privileged scientific standpoint: what people say about why they act is often ideology, and the real explanation lies in material conditions they cannot see. His stock case is the Indian sacred cow — emic account, religious prohibition; etic account, cattle are worth more alive in that ecology.

So the pair carries two opposite valences depending on inheritance:

- **Pike**: emic is the goal, etic is preliminary.
- **Harris**: etic is the truth, emic is what participants believe about themselves.

Contemporary usage is muddier than either. Headland, Pike & Harris (eds.), *Emics and Etics* (1990) is where the two argued it out directly, and is the place to see the disagreement rather than the drift.

### 2.3 It is not insider/outsider

A common conflation, and one that would specifically damage this project.

- **Insider/outsider** concerns the *identity of the researcher*.
- **Emic/etic** concerns the *type of category being deployed*.

An outsider can produce emic description — that is what good ethnography is. An insider can produce etic analysis. Both of this project's central figures make the point:

- **Norbu** is an insider doing etic work in *The Light of Kailash* — a lineage holder arguing Zhang Zhung chronology with a historian's apparatus. The *Talks* and the histories are different registers by the same person. Track which mode any given passage is in.
- **Tiso** is an outsider to Tibet, an insider to Christianity, with an emic motive and a genuinely etic method. If the Steindl-Rast prompt is confirmed, the comparison was framed emically before any Tibetan evidence was gathered — and the fieldwork is still fieldwork. Both facts hold.

Neither person reduces to a position. What gets tagged is the category in use, not the person.

### 2.4 What each can establish

| | Establishes | Cannot establish |
|---|---|---|
| **Emic** | What a community holds; what it values; how it organises meaning; what it wants to be true; how it legitimates authority. Primary evidence for the present-tense community, and for the moment of composition. | What happened. |
| **Etic** | Dates, routes, material contact, population movement, textual dependency, geographic distribution. | What any of it meant. |

The second gap is where Harris overreached. Etic methods can establish that a manuscript is eleventh-century and dependent on an earlier one. They cannot recover what practitioners took themselves to be doing. **Meaning is available only emically.** The etic register is not the truth behind the illusion; it answers a different question.

### 2.5 Three failure modes

**Emic capture** — adopting the tradition's categories as analytic ones. The obvious form is writing "the transmission passed from Garab Dorje to Mañjuśrīmitra" as a historical sentence. The subtle form is worse: **"Gnosticism" was built out of heresiological polemic** — the *opponents'* emic frame — and then naturalised into a neutral-seeming analytic term. That is the Williams/King demolition, and the reason the scare quotes stay.

**Etic imposition** — applying a grid that carves at the wrong joints. Asking whether Dzogchen is "monist or dualist" in borrowed Western metaphysical terms; treating "mysticism" as a cross-cultural natural kind. Most relevant here: **asking whether the rainbow body "is the same as" the resurrection presupposes a neutral third frame from which both can be measured, and none exists.** The question must be rebuilt as: what does each claim in its own terms, and which structural features become comparable once both are described?

**Slot confusion** — the one that matters most in this project. Letting an emic claim and an etic finding compete for the same evidential slot. *"The tradition holds this terma was concealed in the eighth century"* and *"philology dates the language to the fourteenth"* are not rival answers. They answer different questions — what does the tradition hold, and when was this composed. Neither refutes the other. Treating it as a contest yields either debunking or apologetics, and discards half the data either way.

### 2.6 The seam is where the work is

Three configurations, each informative differently.

- **Convergence** — emic and etic point the same way. Bön says its origin is Tazig; material evidence points to an Iranian glory complex. Individually weak; notable if independent. **Independence must be checked before it counts.** Modern Bönpo self-understanding may have absorbed the scholarly identification of Tazig with Persia, in which case the convergence is circular and worth nothing. This check is mandatory before H5 leans on the Bön case.
- **Divergence** — Schopen's whole career. Normative Indian Buddhist texts say monks should not handle money or venerate relics; inscriptions show monks funding stupas and donating for relic worship. The divergence establishes that the texts are *prescriptive, not descriptive* — evidence of what a reforming elite wanted, not of what occurred. That reframing is worth more than either source alone.
- **The pattern of divergence is itself data.** Where self-description departs systematically from the record, ask what work the self-description does. Terma revelation legitimates innovation by attributing it to the past. Naming that function explains something neither the emic claim nor the etic dating explains alone.

### 2.7 Reflexivity: there is no view from nowhere

**The etic grid is somebody's emic.** "Religion," "mysticism," "text," "author," "scripture" are categories with a history, largely Protestant and post-Enlightenment (Asad, Masuzawa, Smith). Etic is not neutral; it is a different emic that has been institutionalised as neutral. This is Smith's finding again at one remove: scholarship presented as disinterested was carrying a polemic.

The response is not paralysis. It is to name the grid and hold it accountable — which is what forcing an `#e/` tag onto every register entry does mechanically, so it does not depend on remembering.

### 2.8 Decision point: the adherent veto

Unavoidable for a project sited at the interface.

- **Wilfred Cantwell Smith's principle**: a description of a religion should be acceptable to its adherents. A strong emic constraint — adherents effectively hold a veto on descriptive adequacy.
- **Lincoln and McCutcheon reject it**: reverence is a religious virtue, not a scholarly one; requiring adherent assent surrenders independence and makes critical findings impossible by construction.

Neither is obviously right, and **they cannot both be held.** Which one governs determines whether a conclusion like *"the rainbow body doctrine emerged in the eleventh century out of Tibetan mortuary practice"* is a legitimate result or a category error. → **Undecided. See §9.**

## 3. Transmission distortion

### 3.1 The general problem

Every source in this project arrived through a chain, and each link can add content. The Norbu translation chain is one instance, not the problem itself.

- **Translation between religious vocabularies.** Italian carries Catholic freight (`risurrezione`, `trasfigurazione`, `corpo di luce`, `realizzazione`) that Tibetan does not. So does English. So did Chinese for the fourth-century Buddhist translators, Sogdian for the Manichaeans, Greek for the Septuagint. The receiving language supplies connotation the source never had.
- **Register shift.** Speech becomes print; teaching-for-an-audience becomes doctrine; provisional becomes canonical. Oral teaching is responsive to a room, and the room is not in the transcript.
- **Reception shaping.** Lopez on Tibetan Buddhism's Western reception; McMahan on Buddhist modernism. A 1982 California retreat transcript sits inside both.
- **Scholarly framing.** Smith on confessional polemic; Williams and King on the construction of "Gnosticism"; the Orientalism debate in its Tibetology-specific form.

### 3.2 The protocol

**Before entertaining a historical explanation for any parallel, exclude the transmission artefact.**

Concretely, for each claimed parallel, ask in order:

1. What is the full chain from the original context to the words in front of me? Name every link.
2. Which link is most likely to have supplied the resemblance?
3. Is the resemblance present in the earliest reachable layer, or does it appear downstream?
4. Does a second translator, from the same source, produce it too?

Step 4 is the practicable test in the absence of Italian: **cross-translator comparison.** Simmons, Reynolds, Clemente, Shane and Lukianowicz all render Norbu into English from different positions. Where they diverge on the same underlying terms, the divergence localises the interpretive layer. Where they converge, the term is probably doing the same work in the Tibetan.

### 3.3 Worked micro-case

"Dzogchen Kazhag Rangdrol" — the terma title Norbu cites in the *Talks*. The chain: Tibetan orthography → Norbu's spoken, Italian-accented Tibetan → an English transcriber's ear → print. Recovering the Wylie is a small, closed instance of the whole problem, and solving it demonstrates the method at a scale where the answer is checkable.

## 4. Dating discipline

Two rules, both mechanical.

**Date both ends.** A parallel between a 1982 oral teaching and a first-century text is not a parallel between traditions until each end is independently dated. The register therefore carries **subject date** and **source date** as separate fields (`resources.md` §0).

**A contact hypothesis must survive its own chronology.** Stated once, because it governs H2: the Central Asian contact window closes in the mid-ninth century, but the rainbow body is prominent in Seminal Heart literature from the eleventh, and Germano locates its emergence in a Tibetan mortuary substrate. Any borrowing argument must explain a two-century gap between alleged contact and attested appearance.

Note the parallel structure in the Mahāyāna case (`background.md` §5.1): texts by the first century BCE, institutional visibility around the fifth century CE. **A long gap between a thing's textual origin and its institutional prominence is normal, not anomalous** — which slightly weakens the objection above, and is worth pursuing rather than assuming either way.

## 5. Strength-of-evidence ladder for diffusion claims

Ranked by what each can support. This is the rubric behind the `#e/` values in the register, and it maps onto the `#r/` facet.

| Rung | Evidence | Supports |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Loanword or calque surviving in the receiving language** | Genealogy |
| 2 | **Datable text in a translation chain with chain of custody** | Genealogy |
| 3 | **Shared arbitrary detail** — non-obvious, with no functional reason to arise twice | Genealogy |
| 4 | **Datable material culture at a documented contact point** | Genealogy (of the artefact) |
| 5 | **Co-presence in a contact zone** | Opportunity only |
| 6 | **Structural similarity of doctrine** | Analogy |
| 7 | **Phenomenological similarity of reported experience** | Analogy, weakly |

**Rungs 1–4 can carry a genealogical claim. Rung 5 establishes only that contact was possible. Rungs 6–7 support analogy and nothing stronger, however striking they feel.**

- **The gold standard**: Barlaam and **Josaphat** — a chain of datable texts through Manichaean, Arabic and Georgian intermediaries, with the loanword still visible in the saint's name (*bodhisattva* → *Bodisav* → *Iodasaph* → *Josaphat*). Rungs 1 and 2 together. That is what a demonstrated transmission looks like, and it runs from Buddhism to Christianity, not the reverse.
- **The field's own caution**: Haber et al., "Ancient DNA and the rewriting of human history: be
  sparing with Occam's razor" (2016) — a warning from inside genetics against reading aDNA into
  simple migration stories. It is this ladder's argument made by practitioners of rung 4.
- **The calibration failure**: the Tarim Basin mummies, read for decades as evidence of Indo-European migration, shown by 2021 ancient-DNA work to be a genetically isolated local population — culturally cosmopolitan, genetically indigenous. **Culture moved; people did not.** Rung 4 evidence had been over-read as demography. For a project asking whether *ideas* travelled, this is both the right tool and the right warning.

## 6. The six deflators

Before accepting any parallel as evidence of contact, rule these out in order. Each has a `#r/deflation` or `#c/` handle in the register.

1. **Transmission artefact** — the resemblance was introduced downstream (§3).
2. **Genre convergence** — origin narratives resemble origin narratives because the genre has constraints, not because the histories touch. (H6; test against Bön, Manichaean and Chan transmission accounts.)
3. **Common ancestry** — both inherit from a third source, so neither borrowed from the other. (H5; the halo case.)
4. **Convergent phenomenology** — similar practices in similar nervous systems produce similar reports without contact. Give this a real hearing, not a paragraph.
5. **Observer framing** — the resemblance is an artefact of the comparativist's position, per §1.1. Ask who framed the question and when.
6. **Circularity** — the emic claim was itself shaped by the etic literature, so their agreement proves nothing (§2.6).
7. **Sampling on the dependent variable** — the pattern was found by looking only at cases that
   exhibit it. Enumerating what generative contact zones share tells you nothing until matched
   non-generative ones are held alongside (Q8a, `background.md` §5.3). The same fault in miniature
   afflicts any parallel assembled by searching for parallels.

A parallel that survives all six is worth pursuing. Most will not survive the first three.

## 7. Handling disagreement

Scholarly controversies are recorded as **pairs or clusters**, not resolved silently in favour of one side.

- Both positions get register entries.
- The item tagged `#e/contested` names its opponent in the `against:` field.
- `resources.md` §16 carries a **controversy register** listing the live disputes and their current standing.
- Where the field has moved, the displaced position is kept and marked — not deleted. Hirakawa's lay-origins thesis is retained precisely because it is the source of the received popular picture, and watching a superseded hypothesis persist in general understanding is itself a §3 phenomenon.

**Negative results are recorded.** A search that fails to find a link is a finding and goes in the register.

### 7.1 Weighting a negative result: archival silence

Not all absences are equally informative, and the difference is systematic rather than random.

An archive is generated by particular people for particular purposes. Where the phenomenon under
investigation is carried by people the archive does not represent — the illiterate, the enslaved,
women, servants, children — **absence of evidence is strongly expected and is close to worthless as
evidence of absence.** This is a predictable bias in one direction, so every estimate of such a
phenomenon's strength is biased downward.

The case that makes it concrete: the household transmission channel (`background.md` §5.4) runs
through caregivers, and in slaveholding societies through enslaved caregivers. They do not write.
The record is made by those who own them. A search of that record for household-level cultural
fusion will under-report it *by construction*.

**Working rule.** Before recording a negative result, ask: *would this archive have registered the
thing if it had happened?* If not, record the absence as **uninformative** rather than as evidence,
and say which instrument would be needed instead. Two live cases:

- **Dunhuang.** A monastic, scribal and administrative archive. It would capture doctrinal borrowing
  and would very likely miss intermarriage, foodways or domestic bilingualism. The "co-presence
  without synthesis" reading (§10, Q8a) therefore cannot rest on the manuscript record alone.
- **Zero-hit database queries** (`resources.md` §17.2). Already weighted this way, and the principle
  is the same one generalised.

## 8. Protocol for adding a source

1. Locate it in the topical hierarchy. If it fits nowhere, that may indicate a missing section rather than an irrelevant source.
2. Assign ≤3 tags per `tags.md`; `#e/` is mandatory.
3. Fill the register fields: subject date, source date, bears-on, access, chain (§0 of `resources.md`).
4. If it makes a comparative claim, assign `#r/` and check it against the §5 ladder. If the claim sits on rungs 6–7 but is tagged `#r/genealogy`, that mismatch is the finding.
5. If it contradicts an existing entry, add both to the §16 controversy register.
6. Mark `[unverified]` unless checked against the source itself.

## 9. Open decisions

2. **Scope of the Iranian material (H5).** How far into Zoroastrian and Sasanian evidence before it becomes a separate project?
3. **Whether `chronology.md` starts now.** §4 argues yes: the dating discipline is currently a rule with no instrument.
4. **Whether to treat convergent phenomenology (deflator 4) seriously enough to require reading in cognitive science of religion**, or to hold it as a standing caveat. The first is expensive; the second is weak.

   but its literature is largely unindexed monographs, and a full comparison class — Palmyra,

   Q8 is answered *for Gandhāra and Dunhuang only*, or pursued generally.
